# The Psychohistory of Moscow: Asimov, Cliodynamics and Eight Centuries of Conflict

*An essay on whether an empire can be "predicted" — and what the modern science of historical patterns can actually tell us*

---

## Introduction: Hari Seldon's question

In the *Foundation* series, Isaac Asimov invented psychohistory — a mathematical science that predicts the behaviour of vast masses of people and, therefore, the fate of empires. His mathematician Hari Seldon foresees the fall of the Galactic Empire and devises a plan to shorten the coming "dark ages" from thirty thousand years to one thousand.

Asimov made no secret of his source: his model for the Galactic Empire was Edward Gibbon's *History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*. From the outset, then, *Foundation* is a book about an empire that sees itself as Rome's heir.

Hence a natural question: can Seldon's lens be applied to a state that literally proclaimed itself the "Third Rome"? Drawing on the key turning points of Moscow's centuries-long conflict with Rus'-Ukraine, can we see a pattern — and perhaps even forecast something?

The short answer: **yes, but not the way Asimov imagined.** That answer is the subject of this essay.

---

## Part I. Why there is no Seldon Plan

Asimov's psychohistory rests on two axioms: the population must be enormous, and it must not know the forecast. But there is also a third, implicit condition that Asimov could not have formulated in the 1940s: the system must be *predictable in principle*.

In the 1970s and 1980s mathematics discovered **deterministic chaos**. A system can obey strict laws and still be unpredictable over long horizons, because a tiny difference in initial conditions grows over time into radically different outcomes. Weather is deterministic, yet a month-ahead forecast is impossible.

Peter Turchin, founder of cliodynamics — the discipline often called "real psychohistory" — says this directly. He acknowledges Asimov's influence but stresses that the precise decades-ahead predictions of *Foundation* turned out to be impossible after the discovery of chaos.

So **modern determinism is not fatalism.** It does not say "everything is fixed and we know how." It says: there are structures, tendencies, recurring cycles — but specific dates, names and endings remain beyond the model's reach.

## Part II. Seldon Crises: the key points of the conflict

In Asimov, "Seldon crises" are moments when history narrows to a handful of possible paths. Let us look at Moscow's history through this frame.

### 1169: the periphery strikes the centre

In March 1169 a coalition of eleven princes assembled by Prince Andrey Bogolyubsky of Vladimir-Suzdal took and sacked Kyiv. Even earlier, Andrey had lobbied Constantinople for a separate metropolitan see in Vladimir — that is, for ecclesiastical autonomy from Kyiv.

But there is an important nuance that fits Asimov's logic perfectly. As historian Oleksandr Aristov shows, Old Rus' texts, including the Kyivan Chronicle, did *not* treat the events of 1169 as a historical watershed. Archaeologically, no catastrophe comparable to the Mongol sack of 1240 can be traced. The significance of a "turning point" was attributed to the event by Novgorodian and Muscovite texts from the 15th century onward.

In other words, **the "crisis" was partly constructed after the fact** — at the moment Moscow needed a genealogy of power. Just as, in Asimov, an empire rewrites its own history to justify its present.

### Late 15th century: expansion after the Horde

Having shed its dependence on the Golden Horde, the Muscovite state in the late 15th century began laying claim to Ukrainian and Belarusian lands belonging to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. Ivan III seized the Siverian lands in 1500–1503. A series of Muscovite–Lithuanian wars followed that lasted for decades.

### Kostiantyn Ostrozkyi: the Mule who did not break the Plan

*Foundation* has the Mule — a mutant whose appearance wrecks all psychohistorical calculations, because one individual changes the course of the masses. The closest historical candidate for this role is Prince Kostiantyn Ivanovych Ostrozkyi (Konstanty Ostrogski).

His biography is dramatic: in 1500 he was taken prisoner by Moscow after the Battle of Vedrosha. Then, on 8 September 1514, as Grand Hetman of Lithuania, he routed the army of Vasily III at Orsha. Contemporaries considered him one of the best commanders of the age; scholars note his innovative tactics — manoeuvring infantry and cavalry together and skilfully using arquebus and artillery fire.

And yet — here is the most interesting part — the decisive victory at Orsha **did not substantially change the course of the war**: Smolensk, captured by Moscow in the summer of 1514, stayed in Muscovite hands.

Asimov's Mule changes history. Ostrozkyi only slowed it down. The structural trend proved stronger than tactical genius. Cliodynamics explains why (see Part IV).

## Part III. How "Moscow, the Third Rome" was born

The ideology that would later become the core of Russian imperial self-understanding did not appear all at once, nor as a political programme.

**1. Background.** The fall of Constantinople in 1453 left the Orthodox world without an emperor. Scholars trace deeper roots as well — notably a 1393 letter of the Ecumenical Patriarch, whose reinterpretation became a catalyst for medieval imperial theories about Moscow.

**2. Formulation.** The formula is attributed to Philotheus (Filofei), elder of the Yelizarov Monastery near Pskov (c. 1465–1542). He set out the thesis in letters to the official Misyur-Munekhin and to Grand Prince Vasily III. Tellingly, these letters are mostly devoted to other, ecclesiastical matters and mention Moscow rather obliquely. Their gist: two Romes have fallen — the first to "Latin heresy," the second to Muslim rule — and Moscow remains the sole bastion of true faith. A fourth there shall not be.

**3. From eschatology to politics.** At its birth the idea was purely religious — a reminder to the ruler of his duty as protector of the faith. It became a political myth of imperial vocation much later; modern scholars link its transformation to the concept of the *Russkiy mir* ("Russian world").

The parallel with *Foundation* is strongest here. In its early phase the Foundation controls neighbouring kingdoms through a "religion of science": power is presented as a sacred mission and submission as salvation. In the same way, Philotheus's eschatological formula turns territorial expansion into a religious duty, and neighbours into people who must be "returned" to the true Rome.

## Part IV. Modern determinism: what cliodynamics says

Cliodynamics is the application of a dynamical-systems approach to history: models built on quantitative data about population, prices, elites, state finances and instability.

### Secular cycles

In *Secular Cycles* (2009), Peter Turchin and Sergey Nefedov develop Jack Goldstone's demographic-structural theory. Their central thesis: agrarian states pass through long cycles — phases of expansion, stagflation, crisis and depression. Strong expansionist periods give way to state breakdown, chronic instability and territorial loss.

A separate chapter of the book is devoted to the **"Muscovy Cycle" (1460–1620)**, with an expansion phase of 1460–1530. Notably, the authors also identify a *preceding*, "Kievan" cycle that ended in the crisis of the 1220s–1250s. That is, within the model itself, Kyiv and Moscow are different cycles, not one continuous "state."

Now let us map the key points onto this scheme:

| Event | Cycle phase | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Ivan III's wars with Lithuania, Vedrosha (1500) | Expansion | The system is growing; resources for war are available |
| Orsha (1514) | Expansion | A tactical defeat does not alter the phase's direction |
| Philotheus's letters (1510s–1520s) | Expansion | Ideology legitimises growth already under way |
| Oprichnina, Time of Troubles (1598–1613) | Crisis | The system overheats; the state nears collapse |

This is why Ostrozkyi "did not break the Plan": his victory fell in a rising phase, when Moscow's internal resources allowed it to absorb the defeat. In cliodynamics a single battle is noise; the direction is set by demography, the condition of the elites and state finances.

### The steppe frontier

In another work, *War and Peace and War*, Turchin proposes a theory of "metaethnic frontiers": states with the highest internal cohesion arise on the border with a radically different world. For Moscow that border was the steppe. Constant external pressure shaped a culture of mobilisation, elite cohesion around the centre, and a tight link between war and the legitimacy of power.

### Path dependence

Another concept from modern social science is **path dependence**. Once a track is chosen, it shapes the next steps: institutions, elite habits and models of legitimation reproduce themselves even after the original conditions have long disappeared. In this logic, the "Third Rome" is less a *cause* of expansion than an ideological superstructure that locked in an already chosen path and made it self-perpetuating.

## Part V. The next crises: from Pereiaslav to Baturyn

If we continue the line beyond the 16th century, the same logic repeats.

- **1654, Pereiaslav.** Khmelnytsky's alliance with Moscow, which Russian tradition would later turn into the myth of "reunification."
- **1658–1659, Hadiach and Konotop.** The Treaty of Hadiach as a project for a Grand Duchy of Rus' — an alternative branch of history. The Battle of Konotop — a brilliant victory that, like Orsha, did not end the war. Another "Mule" absorbed by the structure.
- **1708, Baturyn.** The destruction of the Hetman's capital — the empire's punitive model, aimed not only at an adversary but at the very possibility of an alternative centre.

The recurrence is striking: **individual military victories over Moscow rarely changed the long-term trend** unless they were accompanied by a change in its internal dynamics.

---

## Part VI. The limits of the model

An honest analysis must also name the limitations.

**First, the critique of cliodynamics.** Turchin is criticised for giving far more weight to material factors — the competition for wealth and power — than to ideology, culture or chance. And the subject of this essay supplies a counter-argument: "Moscow, the Third Rome" and the systematic "war on memory" are examples of ideology itself becoming a force that sustains the cycle.

**Second, teleology.** The "Kyiv → Vladimir → Moscow" scheme into which "Seldon crises" fit so neatly was largely created by the Muscovite historiographical tradition itself. By applying a deterministic model to it, one risks unwittingly reproducing the very myth of "inevitability."

**Third, chaos.** No model can say *when* or *how* the current war will end. Anyone who claims otherwise is offering not science but prophecy.

---

## Conclusion: tendencies, not destiny

*Foundation* provides the image. Cliodynamics provides the tool. Chaos theory sets the limit.

Together they reveal not destiny but **the tendencies of a system**: an empire that defines itself through a mission tends toward expansion; expansion gives way to crises; ideology revives in every new rising phase; and memory is not a side effect but a battlefield.

Perhaps the most important conclusion is this: tendencies change when their structural causes change — not merely as a result of individual victories. Orsha and Konotop proved it. The question for the 21st century is not "can a battle be won" but "what will change the trajectory itself."

In Asimov, Seldon believed that knowing the patterns could shorten the dark ages. In this — and only in this — he may have been right.
